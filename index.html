01 / About

Background

I grew up moving. Born in Puerto Rico, then Mexico, Japan, Los Angeles, Puerto Rico again, and finally Dallas. Two of those moves were evacuations, one after the 2011 Tohoku earthquake and tsunami and one after Hurricane Maria. Seeing which things held and which did not, and then starting over each time somewhere I did not understand yet, taught me the same lesson twice: figure out how the system actually works, then go for it. Mechanical engineering was where that landed. I like problems whose answer has to get built and then survive contact with the real world, which is why I have moved toward manufacturing and design. Outside the shop I play soccer and golf for Stanford's club teams.

That has taken me from sand casting and manual machining in the Stanford shops, to soft robotics hardware in the CHARM Lab, to equipment engineering on an etch tool at TSMC Arizona. Different scales, same question: what is actually limiting this, and how do you measure it.

I am finishing a BS in mechanical engineering at Stanford. I am most interested in hardware roles where design, analysis, and fabrication sit close together.

Project 01

Narsil: cast aluminum bottle opener

The problem. Design and manufacture a functional cast aluminum piece end to end, alone, in ten weeks. The hard constraint was the engraving: raised lettering only reproduces in sand casting if the draft angles and pattern geometry are right, and there is no second pour to fix it. I also had no pattern board, so the two halves of the mold had to register themselves.

What I did. Sole designer. Iterated the model in Fusion 360 through foam and machined plastic prototypes before committing geometry, then sand cast the blade, crossguard, and lower handle in aluminum, engineering draft so the engravings pulled clean in a single pour and using cast-in alignment features in place of a pattern board. Turned the 6061 handle on a manual lathe with a radius cutter and machined the mating and assembly features on the mill and drill press using custom soft jaws. When the hardware came in out of spec, I salvaged it in the shop by die-threading, cutting, and belt-sanding the oversized screws to fit rather than losing a week to reordering.

Result. Every cast feature reproduced on the first pour, and the cast surfaces were wet sanded and polished through progressive grits to a mirror finish.

Tools
Fusion 360, sand casting, manual lathe, mill, drill press
Role
Individual project, sole designer
Course
ME 103, Winter 2026

Project 02

Nose What You Need

The problem. Build one grab-and-go station for the things I kept losing on the way out the door: keys, earbuds, four pairs of sunglasses, and a thermos. Two things made it harder than it sounds. A full thermos set in at an angle puts the center of mass well outside the base, so the whole thing wants to tip. And rigid mounts let sport frames slide off, which is worse than no mount at all.

What I did. Drove the structure with generative design in Fusion 360, feeding it real measured loads plus misuse cases rather than ideal ones, which is what forced the wide base. For the glasses mounts I scanned my own face with Luma 3D, cut the nose out and decimated the mesh in Meshmixer, and threaded the result into the PLA base as a separate part printed in TPU, chosen for compliance and a higher coefficient of friction after PLA prototypes let the sport frames slip. The first generative result did not fit the printer bed, so I tightened spacing and gave up two glasses ports to make it printable.

Result. Static stress analysis came back with a safety factor above 15 throughout. The final base printed in a single 13-hour run with no failures, the noses in 6 hours on an Ultimaker, and the whole thing cost about $22 in material. It holds four different frame styles without slippage and stays put with a full bottle leaned at 70 degrees. I still use it.

Tools
Fusion 360 generative design, Luma 3D scanning, Meshmixer, PLA and TPU printing
Role
Individual project
Course
ME 127, Spring 2025

Project 03

Equipment engineering, etch tools

The problem. Etch tool uptime depends on parts that wear quietly. Three questions came my way over the summer: why the wafer transfer arm was aging the way it was, whether it was running on the right lubricant, and whether the mini-environment was actually containing what it was supposed to contain.

What I did. Applied PID control principles to analyze the transfer arm's motion profile, evaluating movement behavior against service life. Characterized candidate lubricating greases using material property data and squeeze flow rheological testing to compare flow and film behavior, which turned lubricant selection into a measured comparison rather than a convention. Analyzed airflow and pressure differential across the fan filter unit and air curtain to assess flow uniformity and containment. Worked daily with process engineering and counterpart teams in Taiwan, across languages and time zones, to align on methodology.

Result. The work advanced to the semifinals of TSMC's company-wide intern project competition.

Tools
Rheological testing, PID analysis, airflow and pressure measurement
Role
Equipment Engineering Intern, Etch
Timeline
TSMC Arizona, Summer 2026

Project 04

Tension reduction devices for a vine robot

The problem. Vine robots grow by everting new material out through their own tip, which is what lets them thread into pipes and rubble. The limit is friction. As the robot unspools past 20 feet, the new material travelling to the tip is fighting gravity and drag along the whole body, and the robot simply stops growing. Tension reduction devices ride inside the body to overcome those forces, and the question was how to make them work well enough, and be measurable enough, to characterize.

What I did. Designed and built the TRDs and the modular tip mounts that carry them, so configurations could be swapped without rebuilding the robot. Modeled in Onshape and Fusion 360, fabricated with 3D printing, ultrasonic welding, and soldering, and wrote Teensy modules in the Arduino IDE so several devices along the body ran in sync off one PCB. Then I built the test infrastructure, which turned out to be half the work: a lightweight, transparent, reconfigurable in-pipe system with 90 degree turns and T joints, using mid-print inserts to capture 6-32 nuts so sections could be bolted into different courses, plus a semi-modular free-space course from printed parts, laser-cut sheet, and aluminum extrusion.

Result. With the TRDs installed, the robot ran 25 feet of pipe through ten 90 degree turns, which is the kind of route that stops an unassisted vine robot well short. The work was presented as a research poster with William Heap and Professor Allison Okamura.

Tools
Onshape, Fusion 360, 3D printing, Teensy, ultrasonic welding, laser cutting
Role
Research intern, CHARM Lab
Timeline
June 2025 to March 2026

Project 05

Stamping clock: a weight-driven timepiece

The problem. The brief was open: build a timekeeping device powered only by a falling weight. The obvious answer is a dial and a hand. We wanted the passage of time to be the machine's physical output instead of a reading you take off it.

What I did. Five-person team. The falling weight drives a gear train into a linkage of our own design that swings a stamp through an arc, from an ink pad onto a moving paper belt and back. Each stamp is one interval. The belt darkens as the day goes on, and a fully dark sheet means the day is done, so the record accumulates instead of resetting every twelve hours. I was in charge of design and fabrication of the mechanisms.

Result. Fully functional, running on gravitational potential energy alone.

Tools
Linkage design, 3D printed gearing, laser-cut acrylic
Role
Five-person team
Course
ME 102, Winter 2025

Project 06

CoralScapes: coral nursery structure

The problem. Coral nurseries grow fragments before they are transplanted onto degraded reef, so a nursery that fails structurally costs a season of restoration work. Ours had to hold four feet of diameter at 20 m depth under drag, wave action, and hydrostatic load, stay easy for divers to attach coral to and monitor, and use materials with low ecological impact.

What I did. Three-person team. We researched four nursery archetypes and rejected the coral tree once we found our site profile did not suit it, then iterated on the fixed-frame bed: a hexagonal frame to spread load through the joints, hexagonal feet to cut the drag coefficient, filleted edges, and holes at the joints for a flexible mesh bed that lets water and nutrients through instead of the rigid steel grid that is standard. I ran the FEA in Fusion 360 and checked it against analytical beam bending by hand, then we printed scaled prototypes and pulled them to failure.

Result. FEA put the minimum factor of safety at 1.45, at the outer legs, and 4.99 on the individual beams. Hand calculations gave 4.6 on the large beam, within about 8 percent of simulation. Physical testing on the scaled prototypes confirmed the joints as the weak point, which is where the model had said the stress would concentrate.

Tools
Fusion 360 FEA, analytical hand calculations, scaled PLA prototypes
Role
Design and analysis, three-person team
Course
ME 80, Winter 2025
